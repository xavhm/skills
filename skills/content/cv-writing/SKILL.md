# CV Writing Skill

Choose **Workflow A** for a standalone resume, **Workflow B** for a specific job offer, **Workflow C** to adapt resume content into a LinkedIn profile, or **Workflow D** for a targeted or general-purpose cover letter. Workflows A and B use the resume specifications and final checks in this file; C and D use their linked guides. Deliver the requested finished copy or document, not just advice about writing one.

This skill closely adapts the article supplied by the user: [Di Reshtei, Resume for Full-Stack Developer: 8 Examples, 60+ ATS Keywords & Section-by-Section Guide (2026)](https://medium.com/@reshtei/resume-for-full-stack-developer-examples-ats-keywords-c70d7b6c82e6). The four-line summary preference comes from the user; the article specifies three to four sentences. Detailed source qualifications are in [full-stack-guidance.md](references/full-stack-guidance.md).

## Working defaults

Use these concrete resume defaults unless the user, application instructions, or a clearly identified content need requires an exception. LinkedIn and cover letters have separate section and length specifications in their guides; do not apply the resume's four-line summary rule to them. Do not ask for approval for routine drafting choices.

| Element | Default to apply |
|---|---|
| Summary | Three short sentences; at most four sentences and aim for no more than four rendered lines |
| Technologies in summary | Three to four defining technologies, selected for the target |
| Evidence in summary | At least one verified metric when available; otherwise a specific supported accomplishment |
| Technical skills | 15–25 relevant, supported skills, grouped by category; use fewer if that is all the evidence supports |
| Recent, relevant employment | Three to five bullets per position, strongest first |
| Older, less relevant employment | Compress to one or two bullets or a compact entry where appropriate |
| Project | Name and stack, two to three concise bullets, and a supplied repository or demo link when available |
| Education after 3+ relevant years | Usually one line per qualification |
| Resume length | Aim for one page below five relevant years; consider two for substantial relevant experience, often seven or more years |
| Five to seven relevant years | Choose one or two pages according to the evidence; do not invent an age-based rule |
| Layout | Single column, standard section headings, important information in body text |
| Body type | Arial, Calibri, or Garamond; 10–12 pt |
| Margins | 0.5–1 inch, approximately 1.3–2.5 cm |
| Delivery format | Follow the application; otherwise DOCX is a practical editable default and a simple text-based PDF is suitable for fixed layout |
| Filename | `FirstName-LastName-Target-Role.docx` or `.pdf`, using actual candidate details |

These are writing and presentation defaults, not guarantees about an ATS. For a non-software role, retain the workflow but replace the technology categories with relevant professional competencies. Do not force this employment-resume format onto an academic or otherwise specialized CV.

## Workflow A — Write a standalone resume

### 1. Collect the minimum useful inputs

Read all supplied material first. Identify the target role or role family, seniority, language/market, and requested output. Extract contact details, dated employment, actual job titles, education, projects, technologies, and accomplishments.

If no target is specified, ask which role the resume should support. A standalone resume still needs a professional direction, but does not need an invented job posting. Ask for missing information in a focused batch; continue drafting sections supported by what is already available.

For each important role or project, collect:

- What was built or changed, and for whom?
- What did the candidate personally do?
- Which technologies and technical decisions were involved?
- What changed as a result: business outcome, user experience, performance, cost, reliability, delivery speed, or scope?
- What numbers are known, and what are their units or measurement periods?

Keep unconfirmed information separate from candidate facts. Never fill a gap with a plausible achievement.

### 2. Select the positioning and evidence

Choose the appropriate approach from the eight patterns in [examples.md](references/examples.md). For full-stack work, prove end-to-end delivery through one coherent feature or project wherever the evidence allows: data storage, service/API, interface, and deployment. Name only the layers the candidate actually worked on.

Select the strongest recent achievements for this role family. Prefer a specific relevant accomplishment over an impressive but unrelated statistic. For a graduate, relevant projects and education may carry the story; for a senior engineer, architecture, technical leadership, delivery, and enabling others should be visible when supported.

### 3. Set the section order and page budget

Start with one of these skeletons, adjusting for the candidate:

- Experienced: Contact → Summary → Technical Skills → Experience → Selected Projects if useful → Education and Certifications.
- Graduate or changer: Contact → Summary → Technical Skills → Projects or Relevant Experience → Education → Other Experience as useful. Move Education higher when the credential is the strongest qualification.

Use reverse chronology within employment. Decide on one or two pages before adding detail. Omit an empty section rather than inventing filler.

### 4. Draft the resume

Write a provisional summary, then skills, experience, projects, and education using the specifications below. For software roles, consult the keyword reference while selecting skills. Draft accomplishments from evidence, using the rewrite patterns when useful.

### 5. Tighten and reconcile

Revisit the summary after the body is written: it should highlight the strongest proof in the actual resume. Remove repeated descriptions, irrelevant early-career detail, unused skills unrelated to the target, objectives, and “references available upon request.” Preserve accurate chronology when compressing older work.

### 6. Run the final checks and deliver

Use the verification sequence below. A standalone resume should be useful across the chosen role family; do not claim it is matched to a particular employer.

## Workflow B — Adapt a resume to a specific job offer

### 1. Read both source documents

Inspect the current resume and the full job posting. Retrieve a supplied job link if its content has not been provided. Do not infer requirements from the job title alone. Preserve the original resume and make a distinct version unless the user requests replacement.

### 2. Build a requirement-to-evidence map

Extract the exact target title, required and preferred technologies, responsibilities, experience level, domain, and any explicit eligibility constraints. In working notes, use:

| Posting requirement or term | Required / preferred | Candidate evidence | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exact wording | Priority | Specific role/project, adjacent evidence, unknown, or confirmed gap | Promote, rephrase, ask, or leave unclaimed |

A missing word with supporting experience is an editing opportunity. A missing qualification is not. Ask about consequential unknowns without holding up unrelated improvements.

### 3. Select the supported vocabulary

For full-stack roles, compare the posting with [full-stack-guidance.md](references/full-stack-guidance.md). Include the posting's exact terminology when it accurately describes the candidate's work. Use supported skills even if they were not the candidate's primary tool. Do not replace a real technology with an unexperienced one to obtain an exact match.

Aim for 15–25 focused skills. Prioritize job-relevant coverage rather than reaching a match percentage. Place defining terms in the summary, list supported technologies in Skills, and substantiate important ones in Experience or Projects. Avoid mechanical repetitions and hidden keywords.

### 4. Rewrite the opening and skills

Use the advertised role wording in the headline or opening when it is a truthful description or clearly stated target. Never change historical job titles to match a posting. Choose three to four central technologies and the achievement most relevant to this employer. Reorder skills categories and their contents to surface the requested stack.

### 5. Select and reorder evidence

Keep employment chronological; reorder bullets within each role by relevance and impact. Replace generic descriptions with concrete examples addressing the employer's needs. Select projects that fill an evidence gap honestly. For a career transition, show the established specialization and real bridge experience without counting all prior years as full-stack years.

### 6. Check substantive fit and factual consistency

Recheck required criteria against the revised resume. Verify that every new term and claim has support; mark genuine gaps only in accompanying notes. Ensure dates, titles, qualifications, and metrics still agree with the source. Do not report a fabricated ATS match score.

### 7. Deliver the tailored version

Run the same document checks as Workflow A. Provide the revised resume first, followed by a short account of the main changes and any remaining fact questions or material gaps. Do not append a long audit unless requested.

## Workflow C — Adapt a resume into a LinkedIn profile

Use the [LinkedIn adaptation guide](references/linkedin-adaptation.md) for section templates, practical length targets, visuals, a worked example, and final checks. This workflow adapts the LinkedIn post supplied by the user about audience, purpose, presentation, readability, and reader empathy.

### 1. Define the reader and purpose

Identify the primary audience and desired action: recruiters considering the candidate, people considering joining their team, prospective clients, potential partners, or professional peers. Reuse stated goals; ask a focused question if the purpose is unclear. A profile can support several uses, but choose one coherent professional positioning instead of trying to appeal to everyone equally.

### 2. Recover the broader professional context

Read the resume, current profile if supplied, and other available career evidence. A resume tailored to one vacancy is a selected view of the career. Recover relevant omitted context from supplied materials or focused questions; do not invent it. Keep titles, dates, credentials, and metrics consistent, while broadening emphasis beyond a single employer's requirements.

### 3. Map the resume into profile sections

Turn the resume headline into professional positioning; expand the summary into an About section; add product, audience, and responsibility context to Experience; curate supported Skills; and identify public projects or work samples for Featured. Draft only requested sections. If the current profile is missing, create proposed copy from the resume without claiming to have audited the live profile.

### 4. Rewrite for the audience

Use accessible language and a natural first-person voice in About unless the user prefers otherwise. Explain why the work matters to the chosen reader. For job search, retain recurring role-family terms rather than optimizing the entire profile around one posting. For clients, partners, or prospective hires, emphasize the corresponding problems, collaboration, or team context. Keep achievements specific and avoid converting goals into claims of existing services or hiring authority.

### 5. Add useful visual proof

Recommend a clear profile photo, a relevant banner, and a small set of credible public work samples when these support the goal. Use short paragraphs, bullets, and optional restrained emojis to help scanning. Keep important meaning in words. Do not make emojis, decorative branding, or new image creation compulsory.

### 6. Check consistency and deliver ready-to-paste copy

Compare the draft with the resume for factual agreement and public suitability. Apply the reference's LinkedIn checks rather than resume formatting constraints. Return the requested profile sections, prioritized skills, and specific visual/Featured suggestions. Separate unconfirmed facts and missing assets from finished copy. Drafting does not authorize editing the live profile, publishing posts, or contacting anyone.

### 7. Keep the profile useful over time

Recommend reviewing it when the user's role, focus, major achievements, portfolio, or professional goals change, even outside a job search. The resume can be tailored per application while the profile remains a current, coherent professional introduction. Do not create a reminder or posting schedule unless requested.

## Workflow D — Write a cover letter

Use the [cover-letter guide](references/cover-letter-writing.md) for paragraph structure, STARR examples, a reusable skeleton, formatting, and final checks. Keep the letter to one page and three to five paragraphs by default. Use the resume as an evidence base; add context and relevance rather than narrating every entry.

### 1. Choose targeted or general-purpose mode

- **Targeted:** Read the job description, resume, and application instructions. Reuse Workflow B's requirement map if it exists; otherwise select the two or three most important needs and map them to actual evidence.
- **General-purpose:** Establish a role family, professional direction, or type of contribution from the available context. Ask one focused question if none is clear. Write a coherent base letter for that direction without inventing a company, vacancy, recipient, or employer-specific enthusiasm. Do not demand a job posting for this mode.

### 2. Identify the argument and examples

Choose one main reason the candidate is worth considering and one or two examples that support it. Draw from employment, volunteering, extracurricular activities, academic projects, or other relevant experience. Select for relevance, not prestige. Connect transferable skills explicitly to the intended work without claiming experience the candidate does not have.

### 3. Add specific employer context when applicable

For targeted letters, inspect supplied employer information and, where needed, current official employer pages. Choose a relevant product, responsibility, customer need, or organizational priority and connect it to the candidate's actual experience or stated interest. Research is not a paragraph of praise: it must explain a reason for fit. Do not invent a personal motivation or block a draft because a hiring manager's name is unavailable.

### 4. Draft the opening, evidence, and close

Put the most useful information first: professional background, intended role, and two or three supported strengths. Develop one or two stories using Situation, Task, Action, Result, and Relevance, with most space devoted to the candidate's actions, outcomes, and connection to the role. Finish with a concise contribution statement, invitation to discuss, and thanks. Use the guide's templates as structure, not facts to copy.

### 5. Format and proofread

For a document, match the resume's contact header and font when available. Use the actual preparation date and verified recipient details appropriate to the format. Check the opening independently, then facts, employer/role names, grammar, repetition, paragraph count, and page fit. Follow the guide's export rules and the application's instructions.

### 6. Deliver the requested letter

Provide the finished letter first. Put unresolved facts or a short customization checklist outside it. For general-purpose work, identify it as a reusable base; avoid claiming it is already tailored to every application. When a specific posting is later supplied, revisit the opening, employer connection, and example selection, not just the company name. Do not submit, email, contact a hiring manager, or create additional application documents unless requested.

## Section-by-section resume writing specifications

### Contact block

Use the candidate's name, supplied location, email, phone, and relevant LinkedIn, portfolio, or GitHub links. Keep contact information in the main document body. Do not invent addresses or add sensitive personal details by default.

### Professional summary

Use this drafting formula:

> [Accurate target role] with [verified relevant experience] building [product/domain] using [three to four core technologies]. [Strongest supported achievement and metric]. [Second achievement or distinguishing capability].

For graduates, lead with the relevant qualification or hands-on project experience instead of inventing professional tenure. For changers, make the transition intelligible. Avoid “seeking a challenging opportunity,” “passionate team player,” and a long technology inventory.

Aim for three short sentences, no more than four, and no more than four lines in the final layout. Four sentences and four lines are different checks. Shorten wording and move secondary detail into the body before changing typography. Plain text cannot establish the final rendered line count. Include a real metric if one is available; do not manufacture one to satisfy the formula.

### Technical skills

Use category labels and comma-separated text, not a flat wall of terms or a visual rating scale:

```text
Languages: [supported languages]
Frontend: [supported frameworks, styling, accessibility]
Backend: [supported frameworks, APIs, authentication]
Databases: [supported databases and relevant data tools]
DevOps: [supported cloud, containers, deployment and version-control tools]
Testing: [supported test tools and methods]
```

Omit empty categories. The 15–25 target is for the section overall, not each category. Every entry should be interview-defensible. Keep relevant older technologies when the job or evidence warrants them; remove irrelevant legacy keywords before current, relevant skills. Demonstrate soft skills through accomplishments rather than filling this section with personality labels.

### Work experience

Use `Job Title | Employer | Dates`, with consistent date formatting. For each recent relevant role, draft three to five bullets using:

> [Action verb] + [specific thing built or changed] + [relevant technologies/method] + [measurable result].

Start with the highest-impact relevant accomplishment. Prefer Built, Designed, Shipped, Implemented, Reduced, Migrated, or Led when accurate. Replace “responsible for” and “worked on” with the actual contribution. Do not promote participation to leadership just to use a stronger verb.

For full-stack experience, connect technologies within a product outcome instead of separating every layer into unrelated bullets. Name the concrete deliverable and the candidate's responsibility. If numerical impact is unknown, look for verified technical results or scope: latency, build time, bundle size, tests, endpoints, services, features, or environments. Use a specific qualitative result when no numbers are available.

### Projects

Format as a small experience entry:

```text
[Project Name] | [Technologies]
- Built [product and purpose] for [actual audience or use].
- Solved [technical challenge] through [decision or implementation].
- Achieved [verified outcome, benchmark or scope].
[Supplied repository or live demo link]
```

Combine bullets to stay within two or three. Personal and academic projects are legitimate, particularly for early-career candidates; label their context accurately. Use real technical measurements when there are no user metrics. Include a useful supplied link when available, never a guessed URL. If a public repository is inspected, check that documentation and the referenced work support the claim. Do not demand disclosure of proprietary work.

### Education and certifications

With three or more relevant years, default to one line per qualification: degree, institution, graduation year or accurate ongoing status. Add coursework or academic detail only when it contributes evidence missing elsewhere.

The article's GPA default is at least 3.8 on a 4.0 scale and graduation within two years. Use that only for the applicable grading system; do not invent conversions. Relevant market or application instructions take precedence.

List relevant certifications by credential, issuer, and date/status. The source suggests AWS developer, Google Cloud developer, Kubernetes administrator, and MongoDB developer credentials as examples. Use the candidate's actual credential name. Check issuer-specific validity before calling it current or expired; do not automatically remove every credential older than three years. Certifications can support a transition or cloud-focused application but do not substitute for experience or become mandatory without an employer requirement.

## Formatting, verification, and delivery

1. **Content pass:** Check names, titles, employers, dates, skills, degrees, contribution scope, and metrics against the source. Resolve contradictions; keep unanswered questions outside submission-ready copy.
2. **Writing pass:** Confirm the summary limits, three to four defining summary technologies, focused skill categories, appropriate bullet counts, strongest evidence first, useful project links, and compact education. Apply the [12-point mistake check](references/full-stack-guidance.md#12-point-mistake-check).
3. **Page pass:** Use the page, font, and margin defaults above. Remove low-value content before reducing spacing. Do not go below 10 pt or 0.5-inch margins to force a page count. Use a second page when relevant evidence justifies it.
4. **Layout pass:** Use a single column and recognizable Summary, Skills, Experience, Projects, and Education headings. Avoid essential text inside tables, text boxes, graphics, headers, or footers. Do not use icons or skill bars as the only carriers of information.
5. **File pass:** Follow the required format. For DOCX/PDF output, use the available document workflow, render the final file, and check for clipping, overlap, awkward page breaks, and the summary's actual line count. Extract text and check the reading order and retention of contact details, dates, headings, and bullets. Selectable text is a basic check, not a complete parser test. Do not claim file checks for a text-only draft.
6. **Completion pass:** Remove drafting placeholders, comments, and accidental tracked changes from a submission-ready file. Use a descriptive filename. Deliver the requested resume, plus concise unresolved issues if any. A draft with missing facts must be labeled as such.

## Essential judgment

Keep the practical defaults visible and usable. Exceptions need a concrete reason, not a blanket retreat into “it depends.” Never invent evidence, force an arbitrary keyword percentage, or promise an interview. Distinguish parser compatibility, role relevance, and human readability; a single score cannot establish all three.
