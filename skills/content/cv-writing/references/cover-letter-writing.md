# Practical cover-letter guide

Use with Workflow D for a letter based on a resume and job description, or a general-purpose base letter. These instructions apply across professions, including candidates whose strongest evidence comes from academics or volunteering.

## Writing defaults

| Element | Working target |
|---|---|
| Length | No more than one page; three to five paragraphs |
| Body word count | Usually 250–400 words; this is an editorial target, not a reason to pad or override an application limit |
| Opening | Two or three sentences that establish the candidate, intended work, and two or three relevant strengths |
| Evidence | One or two developed examples, usually in the second and/or third paragraphs |
| Employer connection | One specific, supported connection woven into the opening or body for a targeted letter |
| Closing | Two or three concise sentences reinforcing contribution, inviting discussion, and thanking the reader |
| Presentation | Match the resume's contact block and font for a document; use readable business-letter spacing |
| Voice | First person, clear, professional, and recognizably the candidate's; personality through choices and examples |

Follow explicit language, length, attachment, and submission instructions first. For a short application field or email body, use the available space and omit unnecessary postal-address blocks. A cover letter is normally prose: the resume's bullet format does not need to carry over.

## 1. Plan before drafting

For a targeted letter, make a small working map:

| Employer need | Candidate evidence | Story to tell | Why it matters in this role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Specific skill or responsibility | Verified experience | One concrete action and outcome | Explicit connection |

Select two or three priorities and one or two examples. Reuse existing job analysis rather than performing it twice.

For a general-purpose letter, substitute the intended role family's needs for the employer column. Use a coherent focus such as customer-facing operations, communications, or full-stack product development. Avoid an unfocused letter claiming suitability for any position. A general-purpose letter can be finished and readable without a fictitious employer or visible company placeholders.

## 2. Make the first paragraph do the most work

Assume the reader may initially read only the opening. Use an inverted-pyramid order: state the most decision-relevant information first, then support it in the body. Do not mechanically cram every who/what/when/where/why/how into one sentence.

The opening should answer:

- Who is this person professionally: relevant role, education, or experience?
- What position or type of work are they interested in?
- Which two or three strengths make the application worth reading?
- For a targeted letter, what specific connection explains the interest or contribution?

**Targeted pattern:**

> [Specific responsibility or verified employer focus] connects closely with my experience in [relevant work]. As a [accurate background], I bring [two or three supported strengths] to the [actual position] role.

**General-purpose pattern:**

> I am a [accurate professional background] interested in [role family or type of contribution]. My experience in [relevant context] has developed [two or three supported strengths], which I would bring to work involving [concrete needs].

Use these as drafting structures. Rewrite for natural voice and remove unsupported clauses. Do not copy example credentials, years, or enthusiasm into the candidate's letter. Avoid openings consisting only of “I am writing to apply” or generic praise when a substantive connection is available.

## 3. Turn accomplishments into STARR stories

Expand a resume accomplishment into complete sentences, adding context and relevance rather than repeating every bullet. Use **Situation, Task, Action, Result, Relevance**:

| Part | What to write | Space allocation |
|---|---|---|
| Situation | The context or problem | One short clause or sentence |
| Task | What the candidate was responsible for | Combine with situation when possible |
| Action | What the candidate personally did and how | Most of the detail |
| Result | The verified outcome, measurement, or observable change | A clear sentence or clause |
| Relevance | How that experience prepares them for the intended work | Explicit connection to the role or role family |

Keep a story to roughly three to five sentences where possible. Do not display the STARR labels in the finished letter. A result can be qualitative; do not invent a percentage to complete the framework. Distinguish the team's achievement from the candidate's contribution, and observed results from predictions.

**Drafting pattern:**

> When [context and task], I [specific action and method]. This [verified result]. The experience developed my ability to [supported capability], which is relevant to [specific responsibility or role-family need].

Choose a second example only if it adds a distinct strength. Two concrete examples are more persuasive than a paragraph listing every skill.

## 4. Use the strongest relevant experience, wherever it occurred

Do not treat paid work as the only valid evidence. These are example-selection prompts, not candidate facts:

| Evidence source | Useful story | Connection to make |
|---|---|---|
| Customer service plus academic study | Handling time-sensitive customer needs alongside analytical or writing work | Explain how these capabilities support a communications or service role |
| Volunteering or student association | Organizing or promoting an event; using actual feedback or analytics to change the approach | Connect to communication, outreach, planning, or audience understanding |
| Classroom group project | Organizing responsibilities, milestones, and check-ins | Connect specific coordination actions to the employer's need for reliable teamwork |
| Technical project | Making an implementation decision and delivering a working result | Explain relevance to the product, engineering responsibility, or user problem |

Identify academic, unpaid, and professional contexts accurately. Mention grades, attendance gains, praise, or measurements only when supplied or confirmed. Lead with strengths rather than apologizing for limited paid experience. Address a gap briefly only when it is central and the candidate has credible transferable evidence or learning to explain.

### Worked STARR example

**Fictional source facts:** A student team had to deliver a research report. The candidate created a task tracker, agreed deadlines with teammates, and ran weekly check-ins. The team submitted the report on time. No grade or praise is known.

**For a coordinator application:**

> During a group research project, I helped coordinate the work needed to deliver our final report. I created a task tracker, agreed deadlines with my teammates, and organized weekly check-ins to review progress. We submitted the report on time. That experience gave me practical experience coordinating responsibilities and following up on commitments, both relevant to the coordinator role.

This converts a classroom experience into evidence without inventing management experience, a grade, or exceptional performance.

## 5. Connect employer research to the candidate

For a targeted letter, use a small amount of useful research. Prefer the job description and current official employer pages for the team's work, product, audience, or priorities. Verify any new current company claim before using it. Do not browse again merely to retrieve facts already adequately supplied.

Use this chain:

> [Verified employer focus] → [candidate's related action or stated motivation] → [plausible contribution grounded in that experience].

For example, an employer's emphasis on customer service could connect to an actual practice the candidate used to help customers. Simply repeating the employer's values does not show fit. Do not say the candidate has always admired the company, shares a personal passion, or knows its internal plans without evidence.

If research is unavailable, write from the posting's actual responsibilities and explain any meaningful limitation outside the letter. A missing company anecdote does not justify fabricated enthusiasm. For general-purpose work, omit company-specific claims and emphasize contribution to the intended type of work.

Do not put research citations or source commentary inside the letter unless requested; any necessary verification notes belong outside the finished copy.

## 6. Finish with a useful closing

Reinforce the main contribution, invite further discussion, and thank the reader. Do not introduce a new unsupported qualification or pressure the employer.

**Targeted:**

> I would welcome the opportunity to discuss how my experience in [area] could support [actual team priority]. Thank you for considering my application.

**General-purpose:**

> I would welcome a conversation about opportunities to contribute to [type of work] through my experience in [relevant area]. Thank you for your consideration.

## 7. Assemble the letter

Use only verified and format-appropriate header fields:

```text
[Candidate's resume contact header]

[Actual preparation date]
[Verified recipient name and title, if available]
[Employer and relevant contact/address details, if available and appropriate]

Dear [verified full name / Hiring Manager / relevant team],

[Opening: professional background, intended role, and strongest reasons to keep reading.]

[First STARR example: strongest matching evidence and its relevance.]

[Optional second example, complementary value, or specific employer connection.]

[Closing: contribution, invitation, and thanks.]

Sincerely,
[Candidate name]
```

If the hiring manager is unknown, use “Dear Hiring Manager” or a relevant team greeting. Use first and last name when verified; do not guess gender or Mr./Mrs./Ms. Avoid “Dear Sir/Madam.” A brief check of supplied or public professional information may help identify the recipient, but do not let an unsuccessful search block drafting or invent contact information.

For general-purpose work, omit unknown employer and recipient blocks rather than leaving fictitious details. Use the actual preparation date for a finished dated document; a reusable template may leave the date as an explicit template field. Preserve required header consistency with the resume without adding missing sensitive information.

## 8. Proofread and export

1. Read the first paragraph alone. It should establish who the candidate is, the intended work, and the strongest reasons to consider them.
2. Check each claim against the resume and supplied evidence; review employer and role names especially carefully after tailoring.
3. Check that each story ends in a relevant connection, rather than leaving the employer to infer it.
4. Remove repeated resume details, unsupported adjectives, generic praise, filler, and unnecessary apologies.
5. Proofread separately for grammar, spelling, punctuation, names, dates, and accidental leftovers from another application. Reading aloud or in a temporarily different font can help; restore the matching font before export. Do not require external proofreading software.
6. For a document, render and inspect the actual one-page result. Match the resume's font and header, use readable spacing, and shorten content before shrinking typography.
7. Follow submission instructions first. PDF is a useful fixed-layout default when accepted. If preparing an email application packet and no contrary instructions exist, a combined PDF with the letter first and resume afterward is a practical option. Use separate files when requested or required by the portal. Do not create or alter a resume solely because the user requested letter text.
8. Check the final export for selectable text, clipping, missing content, and unresolved placeholders. Deliver it with a descriptive filename if a file was requested.

When advising on an application, recommend a tailored cover letter when the channel accepts one and instructions do not exclude it. Do not submit one against instructions or create unsolicited attachments. No letter, typo rule, or customization step guarantees an interview or rejection.

## General-purpose delivery check

A reusable base should still have a specific role-family focus, one or two real examples, and a natural closing. Deliver the readable letter first. Outside it, identify the three changes needed for a later application: the opening/role, the employer connection, and selection or emphasis of the examples. If the user explicitly requested a template instead of a finished base letter, clearly label its placeholders.
